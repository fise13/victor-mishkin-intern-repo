# Inclusive Design Reflection

## Research & Learn

**Who are vulnerable populations, and what challenges might they face in digital spaces?**
People who are more likely to be harmed or left out by how a product is designed: people with ADHD, autism and other neurodivergence, people with disabilities, children, older people, people in a mental health crisis. In digital spaces they can face overwhelming interfaces, confusing navigation, too many notifications, unclear language, and products that assume everyone can plan and remember in the same way.

**Ethical considerations when designing for neurodivergent people**
- Don't overwhelm: fewer options, one thing at a time.
- Respect sensory needs: no flashing, loud sounds or surprise animations, and let people turn things off.
- Be clear and predictable: the same layout and wording every time.
- Don't shame users: no guilt-based streaks or messages that make someone feel they failed.
- Be honest about what the product does and what data it collects.

**Making content more accessible for ADHD and autism**
Simple, literal language; short steps; predictable navigation; visible and low-effort reminders; the option to customize; less cognitive load (don't make people decide more than needed).

**Supporting neurodivergent colleagues**
Be clear and direct in messages (say what you need and by when), respect different working styles and communication preferences, allow flexibility in how and when work gets done, and don't assume silence or a different reaction means a lack of interest.

## First-person account

I read April Jackson's piece on ADDitude, [5 ADHD Organization Tools That Never Work for Me – and 5 That Do](https://www.additudemag.com/adhd-organization-tools-best-worst/). Her main points:
- Many organization and reminder apps didn't work for her. She kept forgetting she had them, and many did the same job.
- Phone calendars helped a bit through alarms, but took too many steps to keep using.
- What worked was visible, low-effort things: sticky notes where she'd see them, a whiteboard, timers, and automation (like an automatic pet feeder).
- Her message: tools are personal, and what helps one person with ADHD can be useless for another.

What stood out to me: a tool that needs ongoing effort to maintain gets dropped, even if it's good. The best reminders are the ones she sees without having to open anything.

## Reflection

**How can I adjust my communication style to be more inclusive of neurodivergent users and teammates?**
Be direct and specific: say exactly what I mean, what I need and when. Keep messages short with clear steps, avoid sarcasm and vague hints, and give people time to answer instead of expecting an instant reply.

**What are common UX or communication pitfalls that might make Focus Bear less accessible?**
- Too many settings or options during setup, which can cause decision paralysis.
- Notifications that pile up or sound like blame (like a list of overdue things), which can make someone avoid the app.
- Features that need regular manual upkeep, which a person with ADHD may drop after a few days.
- Support or help text that is long and written in complicated language.

**One practical change I can make in my work**
When I write messages, bug reports or any text for users, I will use short sentences and one request per message. When I design something, I'll ask "does this make the person decide more, or less?"

## Improvement idea for Focus Bear

Make setup shorter: start with one or two default routines and let people add more later. Nothing is hidden, but the first run isn't a wall of choices. I would check this against the current onboarding when I try the app (issue #8), since it might already work this way.

## Example reply to a user struggling with executive functioning

> **User:** I've been trying to use the app for a week, but I keep skipping my morning routine and now I feel like a failure. I don't even know where to start.
>
> **Reply:**
> Hi, and thank you for telling us. Skipping days is really common, and it doesn't mean you failed. It just means the routine needs to fit you better.
>
> Let's make it smaller. For tomorrow, pick just one step, for example "drink a glass of water", and ignore the rest. If you do that one thing, that counts as a win.
>
> If you'd like, tell us what is the hardest part of your morning, and we can help you set up a shorter routine. There's no hurry, and you can answer whenever you can.
